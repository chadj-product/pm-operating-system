# One-pager

A document whose purpose is to **produce a decision**, not to inform. If nobody has to decide
anything, you want an update instead.

## Structure — in this order, on one page

1. **The ask.** One sentence, first line, before any context. What decision do you need, from
   whom, by when. A reader who stops after this line should still know what you want.
2. **Situation.** Three sentences of context. Assume an intelligent reader with no history on
   this topic.
3. **Impact.** What changes if this is approved — in the units leadership already tracks.
   Revenue, retention, cost, risk, time. Not story points.
4. **Options.** Two or three, each with a real cost and a real downside, including the one you
   are not recommending. **Never present a single option.** A one-option paper is a request
   for a rubber stamp, and experienced readers treat it as one.
5. **Recommendation.** Yours, with the reason, in one paragraph. Take a position.
6. **If we do nothing.** The honest cost of the status quo — including "very little", when
   that is the truth.

## Rules

- **One page.** Enforced. If it will not fit, the thinking is not finished. An appendix is
  allowed; a two-page one-pager is not.
- **Numbers carry units and a source.** Every one.
- **Spell out acronyms on first use**, including the ones that feel universal internally.
  Leadership documents cross org boundaries.
- **Lead with the answer.** No narrative build-up. The reader may be giving you ninety seconds
  in a queue of eleven documents.

## The test

Hand it to someone with no context on the project and ask them what you want and what happens
if they say no. If they cannot answer both in ten seconds, rewrite it.

## Exit condition

One page. An ask in the first line. At least two options with real downsides. A stated
recommendation and a stated cost of inaction.
